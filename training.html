Section one

The training day

Practice is where almost all of your swimming happens, so it is also where fueling matters most often. This section covers the three windows of a training day — before you get in, while you're in, and the hours after you get out.

One

Before training

The job of a pre-training meal is narrow: top off the glycogen in your muscles and liver, arrive hydrated, and do it without leaving food sitting in your stomach. The closer you get to the warm-up, the smaller and simpler the food should be — that single rule explains almost every recommendation below.

  • 3 to 4 hours before

    The real meal

    This is your main fueling opportunity, and the one with the most room for error because you have time to digest. Aim for a plate that is clearly carbohydrate-led, with moderate protein and deliberately low fat and fibre.

    • Carbohydrate is the priority. General guidance for athletes is roughly 1–4 g of carbohydrate per kg of body weight in the 1–4 hour window, with the larger end of that range for the longest, hardest sessions. Practically: a fist-and-a-half to two fists of rice, pasta, potato, oats, or bread.
    • Moderate protein, around 20–30 g. Eggs, Greek yoghurt, chicken, fish, tofu, cottage cheese, or milk. Protein here blunts the blood-sugar spike and keeps you feeling steady, but it is not the star of this meal.
    • Keep fat low. Fat slows gastric emptying more than anything else on the plate. Fried food, heavy cheese, creamy sauces, and large amounts of nut butter are the usual culprits behind a heavy, sloshing stomach in the first set.
    • Keep fibre low. Beans, large raw salads, bran cereal, and a lot of cruciferous veg (broccoli, cauliflower, cabbage) are excellent foods that are badly timed here. Save them for the evening meal.
    • Drink with the meal: roughly 5–7 mL of fluid per kg of body weight in this window — about 350–500 mL / 12–17 oz for most swimmers — so you have time to absorb it and pass what you don't need.

    Examples: rice bowl with chicken and a little soy and cucumber · pasta with tomato sauce and turkey mince · two slices of toast with scrambled eggs and a banana · porridge made with milk, honey, and berries · a bagel with jam plus a yoghurt.

  • 1.5 to 2 hours before

    The compact meal

    Most training days don't leave four hours. Two hours is a very workable window if you scale the portion down and simplify the plate to two or three items.

    • Roughly half the size of the full meal — think one carbohydrate, one small protein, one piece of fruit.
    • Examples: a bagel with jam and a glass of milk · overnight oats with banana · a turkey sandwich on white bread · rice cakes with honey and a yoghurt · a fruit smoothie with a scoop of yoghurt.
    • If you tend to feel hungry mid-practice, this is the window to add a little more rather than eating right before you dive in.
    • Sip 200–300 mL / 7–10 oz of water across the window.
  • 30 to 60 minutes before

    The snack

    At this range you are not building glycogen stores any more — you are just making sure blood sugar is stable and you don't start a hard set hungry. Keep it small, familiar, and mostly carbohydrate.

    • Roughly 25–45 g of carbohydrate, very little fat, very little fibre, little to no protein needed.
    • Examples: banana · a handful of dried mango or dates · a slice of white toast with honey · a plain granola or cereal bar · applesauce pouch · small sports drink · a few crackers.
    • Liquid carbohydrate empties fastest, so a smoothie or sports drink is a good option if solid food this close to a session feels uncomfortable.
  • In the last 15 minutes

    Top-off, not a meal

    • Only needed if you feel low or genuinely hungry: a few sips of sports drink, a couple of dates, or half a banana.
    • Sip water rather than drinking a large volume at once — a full stomach of water is just as uncomfortable as a full stomach of food, and you will spend the first set dealing with it.
    • Finish caffeine, if you use it, well before this point rather than in the last few minutes.

The awkward cases every swimmer actually has

5am practice

Early morning, when there is no time to eat properly

You cannot fit a 3-hour pre-meal before a 5am session, and you should not try. Two things make morning practice work:

  • Treat last night's dinner as the pre-training meal. A proper carbohydrate-containing evening meal, plus a carbohydrate snack before bed, is what actually fuels a 5am session. Liver glycogen drains overnight, so going to bed under-fuelled is the main reason morning practice feels impossible.
  • Then have something small on the way in — 15 to 30 minutes is enough. A banana, a slice of toast with honey, a small bottle of sports drink, a couple of dates, or half a bagel. Even 100–150 calories of quick carbohydrate meaningfully changes how the back half of the session feels.
  • Drink 250–350 mL / 8–12 oz of water when you wake up. You lose fluid overnight through breathing, and almost everyone arrives at morning practice mildly dehydrated.
  • If you truly cannot eat that early, bring carbohydrate to drink during the session instead and make the post-practice meal a real priority. Fasted morning training every day, week after week, is one of the most common ways swimmers slide into under-fuelling.
Doubles

Two sessions in one day

  • The gap between sessions is fueling time, not just resting time. Aim to eat within 30–60 minutes of finishing the first session, then again 1–2 hours before the second.
  • The between-session meal should be carbohydrate plus protein, since it is doing double duty: recovering from session one and fuelling session two.
  • Example day: 6am snack → morning practice → 8:30am recovery breakfast (eggs, toast, fruit, milk) → 12pm lunch (rice, chicken, veg) → 3:30pm small carbohydrate snack → 4:30pm practice → 7pm dinner.
  • Total daily carbohydrate has to go up on double days. This is the most common day for swimmers to under-eat without noticing, because appetite often drops after hard training exactly when intake needs to rise.
Dryland & weights

When lifting comes first

  • Dryland before swimming: eat as you would before any session, and add a little protein (10–20 g) since you're loading muscle and tendon.
  • Dryland after swimming: use the post-swim recovery snack as the pre-lift snack — it covers both.
  • Don't lift on an empty stomach after a hard swim. Strength work done depleted is where form breaks down and small injuries start.
Afternoon & evening practice

The 4pm and 6pm session

  • Lunch is your main pre-training meal. Make it a real one; a small lunch after morning practice is why the afternoon session falls apart.
  • Add a carbohydrate snack 60–90 minutes before you get in — this is the single most useful habit for afternoon training.
  • For late sessions ending at 8pm or later, still eat afterwards. Skipping the evening meal because it's late leaves you starting tomorrow morning empty.

Arriving hydrated

What to actually do

  • 2–4 hours out: 5–7 mL per kg body weight — roughly 350–500 mL / 12–17 oz for most swimmers.
  • Check your urine colour before practice. Pale straw is the target; dark yellow means you are behind, and completely clear usually means you have overshot.
  • Add a little sodium if you sweat heavily, train twice a day, or are coming off a hot session — a pinch of salt in a drink, or a salty snack with the pre-meal, helps you retain the fluid instead of passing it straight through.
  • Caffeine is fine and does not meaningfully dehydrate you at normal doses. If you use it, keep it to what you have tested, and take it 45–60 minutes before rather than at the last minute.

Train your gut, not just your body. Digestion adapts to practice like everything else. If drinking carbohydrate mid-session or eating an hour before makes you feel off, start with a small amount and build it up over a few weeks in training. That is exactly what training is for — and it is the reason competition day should never be the first time you try something.

Two

During training

Swimmers systematically under-drink in practice, because being surrounded by water hides the cues that make you thirsty on land. You sweat in the pool — you just can't see it. A bottle on the wall, used at every stop, fixes most of it.

Under 60 minutes

Water is enough

  • Sip at every wall stop rather than waiting to feel thirsty — by the time you feel it, you are already down.
  • Rough target: 400–800 mL / 14–27 oz per hour, adjusted for how warm the pool and deck are.
60 to 120 minutes

Add carbohydrate

  • Around 30–60 g of carbohydrate per hour once a session passes an hour of real work.
  • Easiest delivery: a sports drink in your bottle, so hydration and fuel arrive together.
  • Alternatives on the deck: fruit chews, a gel with water, diluted juice, a small banana at a set break.
Over 2 hours

Carbohydrate plus electrolytes

  • Keep carbohydrate coming — 60 g per hour at the upper end for long aerobic sets.
  • Add sodium via an electrolyte tab, an added pinch of salt, or a drink that already contains it.
  • A second bottle, one plain water and one with carbohydrate, gives you control over the mix as the session goes on.
Signals

What under-fuelling feels like

  • Times drift in the back half of the session while effort feels the same or higher.
  • Sudden loss of technique, coordination, or focus late in practice.
  • Light-headedness, shakiness, or unusual irritability on the wall.
  • Cramping, headache, or dark urine afterwards — usually fluid and sodium.

The chlorine problem. A lot of swimmers dislike drinking during practice because a mouthful of pool water sits on their tongue. Try a flavoured electrolyte drink or cold water in an insulated bottle — the point is to find a drink you will actually reach for forty times a week, not the theoretically optimal one you leave untouched on the wall.

Three

After training

Recovery has three jobs: put carbohydrate back, give your muscles protein to repair with, and replace the fluid and sodium you lost. How urgently you need to do it depends on when you train next — the tighter the turnaround, the more the timing matters.

  • Within 30 to 60 minutes

    The recovery snack

    • Carbohydrate plus protein together. A practical target is roughly three parts carbohydrate to one part protein, with about 20–30 g of protein in the snack.
    • This window matters most when you train again within about eight hours — doubles, consecutive-day meets, or an evening dryland session. If your next session is tomorrow afternoon, total intake across the day matters more than being fast.
    • Examples: chocolate milk · Greek yoghurt with granola and fruit · a turkey and cheese sandwich · a smoothie with milk, banana, and oats · cereal with milk · rice cakes with cottage cheese and honey.
    • Rehydrate at the same time. Aim for around 1.25–1.5 L of fluid for every kg of body mass lost during the session, and include sodium so you keep it. Weighing yourself before and after a couple of typical sessions tells you your own sweat rate.
  • Within 2 hours

    The full recovery meal

    • Build the plate: a solid carbohydrate base, a palm of protein, vegetables or fruit, and now — unlike before training — fat and fibre are welcome.
    • For a hard session with a fast turnaround, aim for roughly 1–1.2 g of carbohydrate per kg body weight per hour across the first few hours.
    • Add colour: fruit and vegetables bring the vitamin C that helps you absorb iron from plant foods, plus the antioxidants and micronutrients heavy training turns over faster.
    • Examples: salmon, potatoes, and broccoli · burrito bowl with rice, beans, chicken, and salsa · pasta with mince and vegetables · stir-fry with tofu, noodles, and greens.
  • Evening

    The meal that fuels tomorrow morning

    • Dinner is functionally the pre-training meal for a 5am session — treat it that way.
    • A carbohydrate snack before bed (yoghurt and fruit, cereal, toast, milk) tops off liver glycogen and makes early practice noticeably easier.
    • Include a protein source at dinner and, if you can, some calcium — dairy, fortified plant milk, tofu, or leafy greens — since bone loading is the thing swimming doesn't give you.
    • Sleep is part of recovery, not separate from it. Chronic short sleep blunts the benefit of everything above.

Recovery by turnaround time

Don't use training as a reason to eat less. If appetite drops after hard sessions, use liquid calories — milk, smoothies, recovery drinks — rather than skipping.
